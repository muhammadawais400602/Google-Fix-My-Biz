NAP stands for name, address and phone number. A citation is any online mention of those three details, whether on a directory, a social profile, a chamber of commerce page or a data aggregator. When the details agree everywhere, search engines and assistants can be confident about who you are. When they disagree, you pay for it in rankings, in misrouted customers and sometimes in suspended profiles.

Why consistency is worth the effort

Think of each citation as a vote that says "this business exists at this address". Votes that match reinforce each other. Votes that differ, such as "Suite 4" on one site and "Ste. 4" on another, or an old phone number from before a move, split the signal. It also confuses customers: a wrong phone number is a lost job.

Step 1: Define your canonical NAP

Before you audit anything, write down the single correct version and treat it as law.

  • Name: exactly as it appears on your signage and legal paperwork. No added keywords.
  • Address: one format, including how you write suite numbers and directions such as "East" or "E".
  • Phone: one primary local number, in one format. Tracking numbers belong in secondary fields only.
  • Website and hours: one URL and one set of hours, including holidays.
Tip. Keep this in a shared sheet with the date of the last change. When a new hire updates a listing, they copy from the sheet, not from memory.

Step 2: Find every mention

Search your business name, old names, old phone numbers and previous addresses. Check the main directories, the big map and navigation platforms, industry-specific sites and the data aggregators that feed smaller directories. Build a spreadsheet with one row per listing and columns for the URL, what it says for each field, whether it is correct, and whether you control it.

Common offenders

  • Listings from a previous address after a move.
  • Duplicate listings created by an old agency or a former owner.
  • A tracking number used as the main phone on a directory.
  • Abbreviation differences and missing suite numbers.

Step 3: Prioritise

You cannot fix everything on day one, and you do not need to. Rank each problem by how much visibility the site has and how wrong the data is. A wrong phone number on a high-traffic directory comes first; a stray abbreviation on an obscure site can wait.

  1. Your own website and your Google Business Profile.
  2. Major directories and data aggregators.
  3. Industry and local directories.
  4. Everything else.
Fix the source, and the copies tend to follow. Fix the copies without the source, and the errors come back.

Step 4: Fix, merge and remove

Claim and edit listings you can control. For duplicates, claim both, merge where the platform allows, and request removal of the extra. For listings you cannot edit directly, use the platform's correction or support form and keep the confirmation. Note each change and date in your sheet. Be patient: some platforms apply edits within hours, while others take weeks.

If duplicates sit on your Google profile itself, take care. Do not create new listings to replace them, and see our suspension-recovery checklist if any change leads to a verification problem.

Step 5: Add citations deliberately

Once the data is clean, add presence where it counts: directories relevant to your trade, local business associations and reputable community sites. Quality beats quantity. Always paste from your canonical sheet so every new citation starts correct.

Step 6: Maintain it

Citations decay. People move, numbers change, aggregators overwrite good data with stale data. Put a quarterly check in the calendar: re-run your audit searches, spot-check the top ten listings and confirm hours before holidays. Whenever the business changes, update the canonical sheet first and propagate second.

Consistent data is also one of the quieter inputs into map-pack rankings. It will not rescue a weak profile on its own, but it removes a drag that is surprisingly common.
