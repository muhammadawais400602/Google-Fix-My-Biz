A suspended Google Business Profile feels like an emergency because it is one. Calls stop, directions requests vanish and the map listing your customers used yesterday simply is not there. The good news is that most suspensions are recoverable. Across more than 1,200 profiles we have recovered since 2019, the single biggest predictor of success was not luck or inside contacts. It was a clean, complete, well-ordered appeal.

This checklist is the same sequence our team follows. Work through it in order and do not skip the early steps, because an appeal filed too fast is usually an appeal filed twice.

1. Stop and do not touch anything

The instinct is to start editing immediately. Resist it. Every edit made while a profile is suspended can look, to a reviewer, like an attempt to hide something. Before you do anything else:

  • Take screenshots of the suspension notice and the dashboard state, with the date visible.
  • Note the exact wording of the message. "Suspended" and "Not verified" are different problems.
  • Do not create a new profile for the same business. Duplicates are the fastest route to a second suspension.

2. Diagnose the likely cause

Google rarely tells you precisely why a profile was suspended, so you work backwards. In our case files the same handful of causes appear again and again.

Quality-guideline violations

Keyword-stuffed business names, a service-area business showing a street address it does not serve customers from, a virtual office or a mailbox address, and category choices that do not match what the business actually does.

Verification and ownership problems

A failed video verification, a profile that changed hands without a proper transfer, or a former agency that still holds primary ownership.

Suspicious activity

Sudden bursts of edits, many reviews in a short window, or changes to name, address and category within the same week.

Rule of thumb. If you changed your business name, address or category in the 30 days before the suspension, that change is your prime suspect. Revert only when you have evidence the old version was the accurate one.

3. Gather your evidence pack

Reviewers decide quickly and cannot ask follow-up questions, so the pack you submit has to answer them in advance. Collect:

  1. A business license, registration or tax document showing the legal name and address.
  2. A recent utility bill or lease that shows the same address.
  3. Photos of the storefront with the sign visible, plus interior and team photos.
  4. For service-area businesses, a vehicle photo with branding and a list of the areas you serve.
  5. A short video walking from the street to the entrance, if verification is the issue.

Every document should show the same name and address as the profile. If they do not match, fix your source data before you appeal, not after.

4. Fix the profile so it matches the evidence

This is where most do-it-yourself appeals fail. Make the listing an honest mirror of your documents: the real legal or trading name without keywords, a precise primary category, the correct address or a hidden address for a service-area business, and consistent opening hours. Then check that the same details appear on your website and the major directories. Our NAP and citation cleanup playbook walks through that part in detail.

An appeal is not an argument. It is evidence, presented in the order a reviewer needs it.

5. Submit the appeal once, properly

Use the official reinstatement request form from the account that owns the profile. Keep the explanation short and factual: who you are, what the business does, what you believe triggered the suspension and what you have corrected. Attach the evidence pack. Do not write in capitals, do not threaten and do not submit more than one request while the first is pending, because duplicates can reset your place in the queue.

6. Follow up on a sensible schedule

Responses usually arrive within a few business days, although complex cases take longer. Record the case number and set reminders:

  • Day 3: check email, including spam folders, for the decision.
  • Day 7: if there is no response, reply on the existing case rather than opening a new one.
  • If declined: read the reason carefully, correct the specific issue, and only then file a fresh appeal.

7. After reinstatement, protect the profile

Reinstatement is the start of recovery, not the end. For the next 90 days keep changes small and deliberate, add a second owner or manager so access is never a single point of failure, and keep a dated log of every edit. Resume review requests gently and avoid incentives of any kind. If you have been hit by suspicious reviews, read our guide to removing fake reviews safely.

The three mistakes we see most

  1. Appealing before fixing. The reviewer sees the same violation and declines.
  2. Editing during suspension. Changes look evasive and muddy the record.
  3. Submitting mismatched documents. A licence in one name and a profile in another is an automatic decline.
